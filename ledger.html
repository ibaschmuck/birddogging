Forty Years On, He
Still Counts Every Coat

Retired project manager has never once left a job site early. Area painters report “a presence” in the dining room, and a chair that arrives before the crew does.

By MARGARET ELLINGSON · Ledger Staff Writer

The folding chair goes in the dining room, facing the work. It has gone in the dining room, facing the work, for the better part of four decades, and the man who carries it in there has no particular plans to stop.

Terry, who declined to give a last name for this article on the grounds that it “isn’t relevant to the coats,” spent forty years in project management before starting a business of his own invention. Homeowners hire him for twenty percent of their project cost. In exchange he sits in their house from the time the first truck arrives until the last one leaves.

“People think I am there to catch somebody,” he said. “I am there so that nobody has to be caught.”

His standards are written down, which contractors in the area describe as the difficult part. Five rolled coats of paint is the minimum — not, he is careful to point out, the goal. Concrete forms stay twenty‑eight hours. Slump is tested at the truck and recorded before the pour. Lunch is thirty minutes and is taken off the property.

Asked whether that last item was strictly a matter of workmanship, Terry said that it was.

Ray L., a painting contractor with twenty‑two years in the trade, has worked three jobs under observation. He described the experience as “fine,” and then, after a pause, as “very fine.”

“He asked me to open the paint in front of him,” Ray said. “So I opened the paint in front of him.”

Not every job ends in agreement. A Yacolt driveway pour last July went to a second truck after the first tested at six and a half inches of slump against a mix calling for four. The forms came off at nineteen hours regardless. The slab cracked inside the month.

“I wrote it down at the time,” Terry said. “I write everything down at the time.”

“People think I am there to catch somebody. I am there so that nobody has to be caught.” Terry, proprietor

The business has no employees, no office and no advertising beyond a listing in this newspaper. Work arrives by telephone and, increasingly, by way of neighbors who have seen a driveway go in next door.

Homeowners tend to describe the arrangement in similar terms. Karen M., of Battle Ground, engaged him for an interior repaint in the spring and said the difference was plain by the third day.

“I have never in my life seen a man watch paint dry,” she said. “All five coats of it. He brought a chair, and then he brought a second chair for the inspector.”

Contractors are more measured. Several declined to comment for this article. One asked whether Terry would be shown a copy of it and, on being told that he would, declined a second time.

There is a shovel in the truck. Asked about it, Terry said that it was for the yard. Asked again, he said that it had been offered twice. He then returned to the subject of coats, where he remained for some time.

He works alone, travels sixty miles in any direction, and says that he is not hiring.
